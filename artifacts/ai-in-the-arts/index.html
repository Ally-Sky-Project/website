AI in the Arts: Are you creating noise or another overplayed pop song?

Listed 2026-11-03

I’m one week into my experiment as an aspiring digital artist exploring AI for building a world that mostly exists in my imagination. What follows are my reflections as I’m processing this new experience.

I’m new to making AI art but I’m not new to using AI for technical work. The purpose of this post is to reflect on my own experiences: what I think works, what I find inspiring, and what I think is the core dilemma: are we making noise or overplayed pop songs? It is also an invitation to you to share your own experiences as you reflect back on your own trajectory.

Why practice art?

I believe that it is intrinsically rewarding for us to create art and share it with others. That is we simply create art because it is enjoyable to do so and it enables us to form connections with others. It’s a vulnerable process because your art is a reflection of you to some extent and invites room for both criticism and praise. With AI, if the art is not to other people’s liking, it is tempting to lay blame on the technology itself and disregard our responsibility in how we interact with this new and strange thing.

AI as instrument

There are two components to a piece of art: the underlying artistic vision and the execution. In my brief encounter with AI art, I have mainly explored at the executive level. That is, I provide the vision and the AI acts as an assistant or workhorse that executes my vision based on the description that I provide. It is a constant battle with the machine, trying to pull it away from the generic without triggering some quirk. And I’m on the losing side. If we take making a comic series as an example, I start out with writing a script in my own words and my own ideas. Then once I produce the visuals for it, I end up with the most safe and generic AI graphics. My scenes are also littered with tropes and glitches like exaggerated expressions that don't match the writing, objects appearing and disappearing, and people teleporting across the room between frames.

AI transparency

To openly admit that you are using AI means that you will only share your work with a small group of people because most communities are anti-AI. With the power and flexibility of these models, some AI-generated content cannot easily be distinguished from human content. I’m seeing that if you post to any of these large communities and your work has some tell-tale sign of being AI-generated, you are likely to face backlash. It makes me wonder about the kind of strange social game dynamics that have emerged. Simply put, it’s a game of cat and mouse. On one end you have those who train themselves to recognize AI-generated content and on the other end you have those who try to hide their use of AI. As for me, I want to disclose how and where I use AI because I don’t think I deserve credit for something that I didn’t do. That said, even if I didn't create all of the art, I take responsibility for the final result. It’s not “AI slop.” It’s my slop.

AI uniqueness and style

Given that AI lowers the bar to entry to draw professional-looking art, it opens up the doors to virtually anyone with AI access. While that is true, it seems to me that much of the AI being produced seems to have a generic style. Perhaps it is so that it becomes harder to create and find your own unique voice as a result. As of now, I don’t think I have found mine. What I can create looks generic, but I still think it looks a lot better than what I can currently do without tracing the AI’s work with my iPad pen. So, it is easy to gravitate towards that style because it feels safe and the effort to produce it is low. I tell myself as long as the writing is mine that artistic visuals matter less - what a foolish lie.

AI for inspiration

I find that there’s something truly satisfying about having a creative vision being brought to life by AI. I’m talking about taking some idea you had, like an idea for a visual art piece, or a rough song that you recorded, then plugging it into one of these generative models and then after several attempts and tweaks having it produce a result that’s very closely aligned with what you imagined in your head. It’s a weird instant gratification that side-steps all the friction and frustration trying to bring your vision to life in the traditional way. It completely short circuits the work that you need to put in in order to produce quality based on the so-called 10,000-hour rule. This rule suggests that to become an expert at something you need to spend about 10,000 hours of intense effort learning and practicing to develop the skill.

If there’s something that you have created with AI that you truly enjoy and can’t make yourself, you now have the opportunity to study the piece and break down how it was constructed. You can pick some particular aspect that you like and try to reproduce it. Perhaps it is how the kick drum sounds and interacts with the bass line in one of your Suno tracks. Ok, great! You can then get the stems as WAV and MIDI. Next, study them, and see if you can reproduce this particular part. You can consult the classical learning resources as well as AI models that may guide you on your way. See if you can distill down the fundamental principles that make the whole thing work. Finally, let these principles be your guiding light and work together with the AI to play by them to create the effect that you are after.

AI in the arts

What matters first and foremost is your vision of what you want to create and don’t sacrifice that for recognition. Picasso wanted to unlearn his training and paint with the freedom and creativity of the child. Monet wanted to capture the glittering glow of reality in the moment on canvas. Early on, few people liked his work and so it was difficult for him to sell it. Eventually, Monet became both famous and successful without ever compromising his vision.

Artistic taste is subjective and grounded in personal experience, cultural background, and preprogrammed neurology. I believe it is a reflection of the brain’s limited ability to compress and perceive reality. We all walk around with a prediction machine that tries to forecast the next event, whether that’s the next note in music or the next step you take. The brain’s prediction layer interacts with a statistical model of your summed experiences; art included - it decides what you think is a beautiful painting or what’s a beautiful piece of music. The model gets updated as you acquire new experiences. It’s a circuitry built on surprise; too much surprise and the perception is discomfort - noise. Not enough surprise, the prediction error is low, and the reward - dopamine is low. Listen to the same song 100 times over and what you once thought was an amazing song gradually fades away. That’s the inevitable consequence of having a prediction machine that automatically learns to predict through repetition.

When AI is asked to produce art via diffusion models via generic prompt, the output is a professional-looking, safe, and generic style. It’s an overplayed pop song simply because it will gravitate towards its statistical center when engaged in that way.

What is the art of the future that carefully walks the line and hits that sweet spot? What role does AI play in that for you? Today, AI is my assistant, - a blazing fast and accurate coder, artist, and production partner. It makes me wonder what new and creative ways it takes to open up doors beyond our imagination that breaks away from the generic and safe? Does it matter to you if you are alone on the journey and no one except you appreciates the art? Is the lack of recognition a sign that you are not pleasing people’s current perception because they have not reached a point where their statistical model is ready to appreciate it, or does it lack appreciation because your work lacks imagination?
